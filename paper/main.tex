\author{Anonymous Submission}

\begin{abstract}
Symmetries can compress game representations, accelerate equilibrium computation, and constrain decentralized coordination, but exact payoff invariance is brittle under agent heterogeneity, estimation noise, and design perturbations.  We study finite normal-form games equipped with a candidate player--action relabeling group and define an approximate-symmetry defect through unilateral-deviation incentives rather than raw payoff differences.  The defect is invariant to strategically irrelevant utility shifts.  We give a sparse linear program for projection onto the nearest exactly symmetric game and an equivalent maximum-mean-cycle algorithm whose critical cycle is a local certificate of unavoidable asymmetry.  We prove that an $\epsilon$-Nash equilibrium of a surrogate at defect $\delta$ is an $(\epsilon+\delta)$-Nash equilibrium of the original game; consequently every game admits a symmetry-respecting $\delta_G$-equilibrium, with an asymptotically tight coefficient.  Group averaging is a closed-form factor-two approximation to the optimal strategic projection, and we construct a family showing that this factor is asymptotically tight.  For two-player zero-sum games, orbit-restricted linear programs directly minimize saddle-point residual and yield exploitability certificates, which we extend to sampled payoff tables.  Laptop-scale experiments verify the sharp constants and certificates, separate strategic from payoff asymmetry, and quantify the compression--fidelity trade-off.
\end{abstract}
