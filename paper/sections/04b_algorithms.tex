\section{Algorithms and Model Selection}

The theory gives two complementary projection methods.  The sparse LP handles additional linear structure such as zero-sum or common payoffs.  The cycle solver applies to the unrestricted defect and additionally returns a local obstruction witness.  Once a surrogate is constructed, the equilibrium program is selected by the operational setting: Nash for independent play, CE/CCE when a correlation device is available, and the saddle-gap LP for two-player zero-sum auditing.

For a nested hierarchy $G_0\subseteq\cdots\subseteq G_K$, the defect is monotone while orbit counts decrease.  A simple certified rule is therefore
\[
\max\{k:\delta_{G_k}(\Gamma)\le\varepsilon_{\rm sym}\},
\]
which chooses the most compressed candidate satisfying a prescribed symmetry-error budget.  The generator and Reynolds bounds can screen candidates before the sharp solve, and the critical cycle can suggest which orbit to split when the budget is violated.

The final guarantee decomposes cleanly.  If a payoff estimate $\widetilde\Gamma$ is within strategic distance $\varepsilon_{\rm stat}$ of the population game, a surrogate is solved to equilibrium error $\varepsilon_{\rm solve}$, and its empirical projection defect is $\widehat\delta_G$, then
\begin{equation}
\text{original-game violation}
\le
\varepsilon_{\rm solve}
+
\widehat\delta_G
+
\varepsilon_{\rm stat}.
\label{eq:error-decomposition}
\end{equation}
The same decomposition applies to Nash, CE, and CCE; in zero-sum games, summing the two player regrets gives the saddle-gap certificate.  Thus approximation from optimization, symmetry enforcement, and payoff estimation can be budgeted and reported separately.
