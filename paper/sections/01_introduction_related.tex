\section{Introduction}

Symmetry is one of the most useful forms of structure in a multi-agent system.  Interchangeable agents, equivalent roles, repeated resources, and relabeled actions can collapse a large game into a much smaller quotient.  Symmetry can also serve as a principled equilibrium-selection constraint when independently designed agents must coordinate without a privileged labeling.  Exact equality, however, is brittle.  Nominally identical robots have different energy costs, estimated payoff tables contain noise, and game designers routinely introduce small role- or location-dependent perturbations.  A single such change can destroy every literal automorphism even when the strategic interaction remains nearly unchanged.

Measuring the resulting error directly in payoffs is unsatisfactory.  Adding an arbitrary function $c_i(a_{-i})$ to player $i$'s utility can move the payoff tensor arbitrarily far while leaving every unilateral incentive, best response, Nash equilibrium, correlated equilibrium, and learning dynamic based on regret unchanged.  What matters strategically is not the absolute height of each payoff entry, but the differences a player can create by changing its own action while opponents are fixed.

We therefore study a finite game $\Gamma$ together with a candidate finite group $G$ of player and action relabelings.  The group may come from known interchangeability, a game designer's intended semantics, or a hierarchy of increasingly aggressive abstractions.  Our objective is to turn $G$ into a \emph{certified abstraction}: project $\Gamma$ onto an exactly $G$-invariant surrogate $\widehat\Gamma$, solve the surrogate in its orbit representation, and translate the solution back to the original game with an explicit regret certificate.  The central quantity is the minimum change in any unilateral-deviation incentive required to make $G$ exact, which we call the \emph{strategic symmetry defect} $\delta_G(\Gamma)$.

This viewpoint separates three questions that are conflated by a binary exact-symmetry test.  First, \emph{fidelity}: how much do incentives change when the symmetry is enforced?  Second, \emph{computation}: how many orbit variables and constraints can be removed?  Third, \emph{coordination}: should agents use independent invariant strategies, or can a symmetry-respecting correlation device coordinate their roles? Our contributions are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We define approximate game symmetry through maximum pairwise difference (MPD), a seminorm that quotients out strategically irrelevant payoff components.  Zero strategic defect means strategic equivalence to a symmetric game, not literal equality of payoff tables.
    \item We give a sparse linear program for the nearest exactly $G$-invariant surrogate.  More structurally, the unrestricted projection is a potential-fitting problem on a directed graph of payoff-coordinate orbits: $\delta_G$ equals a most-negative mean cycle.  The critical cycle is an independently checkable lower-bound witness, while shortest-path potentials construct a matching surrogate.
    \item We prove additive equilibrium transfer.  An $\epsilon$-Nash equilibrium of a surrogate at strategic distance $\delta$ is an $(\epsilon+\delta)$-Nash equilibrium of the original game.  Every finite game therefore admits a $G$-respecting $\delta_G$-equilibrium, and the coefficient one is asymptotically tight.
    \item We extend the same guarantee to correlated and coarse-correlated equilibrium.  In an exactly symmetric game, averaging over the group preserves both equilibrium feasibility and every invariant linear objective.  This yields a polynomial-time profile-orbit LP for welfare-optimal invariant CE in general-sum games and a sharp role-assignment separation between independent and correlated symmetry-respecting play.
    \item We analyze the Reynolds, or group-averaging, projection.  It is available in closed form from payoff-coordinate orbits and lies within a factor two of the optimal strategic projection.  An explicit zero-sum family approaches the factor two, while nested groups induce a certified compression--fidelity frontier.
    \item For two-player zero-sum games, we formulate orbit-restricted linear programs that directly minimize saddle-point residual and provide exploitability certificates.  We also extend the guarantees to payoff tables estimated from bounded samples.
    \item Laptop-scale experiments verify the sharp constants and critical-cycle witnesses, calibrate Nash and correlated-equilibrium certificates, separate strategic from payoff-level asymmetry, and quantify both computational compression and the welfare value of correlation.
\end{itemize}
