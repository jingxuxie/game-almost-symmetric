\section{Discussion and Limitations}

Our guarantees are deliberately incentive-level.  A small strategic defect implies that enforcing the proposed symmetry yields a profile with small unilateral regret, but it does not imply that every invariant equilibrium has high welfare or that equilibrium selection is stable.  Even an exactly symmetric coordination game can have both efficient asymmetric equilibria and inefficient symmetric equilibria.  The role-assignment experiment makes this distinction visible.  Additional structure---for example, a unique equilibrium, strict payoff margins, monotonicity, or a specified welfare-selection rule---is required to convert regret preservation into welfare preservation.

The candidate group $G$ is supplied.  This is natural when symmetries are suggested by interchangeable agents, repeated roles, or known design transformations, and it keeps the statistical and computational certificates auditable.  Discovering the best approximate relabeling group is a harder combinatorial problem.  The cycle witness from Theorem~\ref{thm:cycle} may be useful for that task: it identifies the small collection of orbit identifications and unilateral comparisons responsible for the current defect, suggesting split-or-merge heuristics for refining a candidate group.

The projection algorithms operate on explicit normal-form tables.  Their complexity is polynomial in the table size but therefore exponential in the number of players for compactly represented games.  Extending the orbit and cycle constructions to polymatrix, graphical, stochastic, and extensive-form games is an important direction.  In particular, one would like a local strategic defect that composes over factors or information sets rather than materializing every joint action.

General-sum equilibrium computation remains difficult after projection.  Our general theorem establishes existence and transfer, while the strongest end-to-end algorithms use two-player zero-sum games.  Correlated equilibrium, coarse correlated equilibrium, and potential-game subclasses may provide tractable general-sum extensions because their equilibrium conditions are linear or convex.

Finally, the finite-sample experiment uses independent bounded observations for every payoff entry.  Real data are often nonuniform, adaptive, or trajectory-generated.  Confidence regions tailored to observed coverage could be inserted into the same projection-and-transfer pipeline, but obtaining tight certificates under selective observation is a separate statistical problem.

\section{Conclusion}

We developed a quantitative theory of almost symmetric finite games.  Measuring relabeling error through unilateral incentives yields a strategically invariant defect, an exact LP, and a maximum-mean-cycle characterization with a local obstruction certificate.  The defect transfers equilibria with an optimal additive constant; orbit averaging gives a fast factor-two approximation; and zero-sum orbit programs turn symmetry into both computational compression and certified exploitability bounds.  These results provide a principled way to exploit symmetry when exact equality is an unrealistic modeling assumption.
