\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\paragraph{How to use the framework.}
A practical workflow starts from a small hierarchy of semantically plausible groups rather than attempting unconstrained symmetry discovery.  Generator discrepancies and the Reynolds upper bound in \eqref{eq:screening-bounds} cheaply reject poor candidates.  For the remaining groups, the LP or cycle algorithm computes the sharp defect and a critical witness.  The designer then selects the largest group within an acceptable incentive budget, solves the corresponding quotient game, and reports both the a priori transfer bound and the realized a posteriori regret.  This makes the abstraction decision auditable: the group, the coordinates it merges, the obstruction cycle, and the final error are all explicit.

\paragraph{Independence versus correlation.}
A symmetry-respecting Nash profile and a symmetry-respecting correlated distribution encode different operational assumptions.  The first requires agents to randomize independently from identical or relabeled marginals.  The second permits a public or private coordination device whose distribution itself is invariant.  Proposition~\ref{prop:role-correlation} shows that this distinction can be decisive: enforcing symmetry need not reduce welfare when correlation is available.  In applications, the CE result is appropriate when agents share a random seed, receive assignments from a dispatcher, or can condition on a common signal.  The Nash result remains the relevant certificate when such coordination is unavailable.

\paragraph{What the defect does not guarantee.}
Our guarantees are deliberately incentive-level.  A small strategic defect ensures that enforcing the proposed symmetry yields small unilateral deviation gains, but it does not imply that every invariant equilibrium has high welfare, that equilibrium selection is stable, or that payoff levels are close.  Nonstrategic utility components can change welfare while leaving the defect zero.  Additional assumptions---for example, a specified linear selection objective, strict equilibrium margins, uniqueness, or a welfare-stable equilibrium correspondence---are needed to transfer value rather than incentives.  The correlated-equilibrium LP partly addresses selection by optimizing an explicit objective over the certified feasible set, but it does not create a welfare guarantee for the original game without further structure.

\paragraph{Representation and computational scope.}
The candidate group $G$ is supplied.  This is natural when interchangeability comes from known roles, duplicated resources, or design transformations, and it keeps certificates interpretable.  Discovering the best relabeling group is a harder combinatorial problem.  Critical cycles may support refinement heuristics: repeatedly split an orbit or remove a generator implicated in the current obstruction.  The projection algorithms are polynomial in an explicit normal-form table and therefore exponential in the number of players for compact representations.  Extending the incentive graph to polymatrix, graphical, stochastic, and extensive-form games will require local defects that compose over factors or information sets.

General-sum Nash computation remains difficult after projection.  The CE and CCE extension supplies a polynomial-time general-sum endpoint because their incentive regions are polyhedral, but this is a different solution concept and assumes access to correlation.  Finally, our finite-sample analysis uses independent bounded observations for every payoff entry.  Nonuniform, adaptive, or trajectory-generated data call for coverage-aware confidence regions.  Such regions can be inserted into the same projection-and-transfer argument, but obtaining sharp partial-information certificates is a separate statistical problem.

\section{Conclusion}

We developed a quantitative theory of almost symmetric finite games.  Measuring relabeling error through unilateral incentives yields a strategically invariant defect, an exact sparse LP, and a maximum-mean-cycle characterization with a local obstruction certificate.  The defect transfers Nash, correlated, and coarse-correlated equilibria with an optimal additive coefficient; orbit averaging gives a fast factor-two approximation; and orbit programs turn approximate symmetry into computational compression with explicit incentive guarantees.  The resulting framework distinguishes the fidelity of a proposed symmetry from the coordination resources used to realize it, providing a principled way to exploit symmetry when exact equality is an unrealistic modeling assumption.
